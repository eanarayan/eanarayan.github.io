Work

Physics, instrumentation, and computing.

My work ranges from studying some of the rarest processes produced at the Large Hadron Collider to developing the electronics and detector technologies required to measure them.

01 · HIGGS PHYSICS

Searching for Higgs-boson pair production.

At SMU, I work on the ATLAS search for events in which two Higgs bosons are produced together.

My work focuses on the bbττ channel: one Higgs boson decays into two bottom quarks, while the other decays into two tau leptons.

Because Higgs-pair production is extremely rare, the analysis relies on sophisticated event reconstruction, statistical techniques, and machine-learning methods to distinguish signal events from much larger backgrounds.

02 · DETECTOR ELECTRONICS

Making sure data survive the journey out of the detector.

At the center of the ATLAS detector, the pixel detector records the trajectories of particles produced in proton-proton collisions.

Those electrical signals then have to travel several metres through cables before reaching downstream readout systems. Along the way, the signals become distorted.

The Gigabit Cable Receiver (GBCR) is a custom integrated circuit designed to restore that signal quality so the detector data can be read reliably.

At SMU, I built and operated a post-silicon validation platform for these chips and have worked on high-speed signal testing, device qualification, debugging, and radiation-reliability studies.

03 · B-PHYSICS

Precision measurements of neutral B mesons.

During my Ph.D. at the University of New Mexico, I worked on precision measurements involving B mesons using data collected by the ATLAS experiment.

I contributed directly to the measurement of the lifetime of the neutral B0 meson, developing analysis techniques for reconstructing events and extracting the particle lifetime from collision data.

The resulting ATLAS measurement achieved exceptional precision and became an important reference measurement in heavy-flavor physics.

04 · SILICON DETECTORS

Building detectors for the High-Luminosity LHC.

Another major part of my research has involved silicon particle detectors — the technology used to precisely reconstruct charged-particle trajectories inside ATLAS.

As a visiting research fellow at IJCLab, University of Paris-Saclay, I contributed to the assembly, testing, and quality assurance of silicon pixel modules for the ATLAS Inner Tracker upgrade.

My doctoral research also included modeling how radiation affects silicon detectors over time, helping us understand how detector performance evolves in the challenging radiation environment of the LHC.
